📖 Learn — Detailed Study Notes

4.1 Magnetic and Non-magnetic Materials

Some materials are attracted towards a magnet while others are not. Materials attracted towards a magnet are called magnetic materials; iron, nickel and cobalt are examples. Materials not attracted towards a magnet are called non-magnetic materials. Common magnets may be bar, U-shaped or ring magnets, and naturally occurring magnets are called lodestones.

4.2 Poles of a Magnet

When iron filings are placed around a bar magnet, most collect near its two ends. These ends are the North pole and South pole. A magnet always has its poles in pairs. Breaking a magnet produces smaller magnets, each with both North and South poles; an isolated single pole cannot be obtained.

Remember: Poles are the regions where a magnet's attraction for magnetic materials is strongest.

4.3 Finding Directions

A freely suspended magnet comes to rest along the north-south direction. The end pointing north is called the North-seeking/North pole and the other end is the South-seeking/South pole. This happens because Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet.

A magnetic compass contains a freely rotating magnetised needle. Its needle indicates the north-south direction and the dial can then be used to identify the other directions.

🧭 Making a Simple Compass

The chapter describes magnetising an iron sewing needle by repeatedly stroking it with one pole of a permanent magnet. The magnetised needle can be passed through a cork and floated in water. When it settles, it aligns in a consistent direction and can act as a simple compass.

Historically, an Indian navigation device called matsya-yantra (machchh-yantra) used a magnetised fish-shaped iron piece kept in oil.

4.4 Attraction and Repulsion

Unlike poles attract: North–South attract each other.
Like poles repel: North–North and South–South repel each other.

Both ends of an ordinary iron bar can be attracted by either pole of a magnet. Therefore, repulsion is a reliable property for identifying a magnet; attraction alone is not sufficient because iron can also be attracted.

🧲 Magnetic Effect Through Materials

The chapter investigates placing wood, cardboard, plastic and glass between a magnet and a compass needle. There is no appreciable change in the needle's deflection, showing that magnetic effect can act through these non-magnetic materials.

🎉 Fun with Magnets & Safety

  • Magnets can move suitable objects without touching them.
  • They can help move steel balls through a maze or retrieve a steel paper clip from water without touching it directly.
  • Like poles can make magnet-powered objects move apart.
  • Do not heat, drop or hammer magnets.
  • Store magnets properly, keeping unlike poles on the same side and using suitable keepers where appropriate.

Application & HOTS

  1. Three identical metal bars contain two magnets and one iron bar. How can you identify the two magnets without another material?
  2. How can you find the poles of an unmarked magnet using a marked magnet?
  3. Why is repulsion better than attraction for identifying a magnet?
  4. A mechanic's steel screws keep falling from a screwdriver. Suggest a magnetic solution.
  5. Why does a compass needle still respond when wood or glass is placed between it and a magnet?

Activity-Based Practice

  1. Predict which classroom objects will be attracted to a magnet, then test and record your observations.
  2. Use iron filings to investigate where magnetic attraction is strongest on a bar magnet.
  3. Repeat a freely suspended magnet experiment several times and record the direction in which it settles.
  4. Compare the behaviour of like and unlike poles.
